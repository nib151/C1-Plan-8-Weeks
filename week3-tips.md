The three areas that define this gap at C1 are:

- **Lexical range and collocation accuracy** (not just knowing words, but deploying them correctly)
- **Discourse coherence in writing and speaking** (structuring arguments that feel *inevitable*, not just logical)

These aren't weaknesses to be ashamed of — they're the exact frontier where C1 is won or lost.

## 🛠️ Five Specific Improvement Strategies for Week 3

### 1. 🔤 Collocation Drilling — Move Beyond Single Words
At C1, examiners aren't just checking vocabulary — they're checking **how naturally you combine words**. Using "make a decision" is fine; using "reach a considered decision" signals C1 mastery.

**Today's micro-task:** Take any 5 nouns from a topic you'll likely encounter (e.g., *change, impact, approach, challenge, role*) and write **three collocations** for each using a collocation dictionary (ozdic.com is excellent). Then write one sentence per collocation. Time yourself: 15 minutes maximum. The speed constraint forces automaticity.

---

### 2. ✍️ Paragraph Reverse-Engineering for Writing Coherence
Most C1 writing errors aren't grammatical — they're **structural**. Paragraphs that list ideas rather than *develop* them cost you marks in every written task.

**Today's micro-task:** Find a high-quality opinion article (The Economist, The Guardian's long reads). Take **one body paragraph** and strip it down to its skeleton: *Topic sentence → Development → Evidence/Example → Evaluative comment*. Then write your own paragraph on a different topic using the **exact same skeleton**. This trains you to think architecturally, not just linguistically.

---

### 3. 🗣️ Spoken Complexity — The "Upgrade" Drill
In speaking tasks, candidates under pressure revert to simple syntax. This drill rewires that habit.

**Today's micro-task:** Record yourself speaking for **90 seconds** on any topic (e.g., "Is remote work better than office work?"). Play it back and identify every simple sentence — any sentence with just one clause. Rewrite and re-record those sentences using **subordination, concession, or hedging** (e.g., *"While remote work offers flexibility, it arguably undermines the spontaneous collaboration that drives innovation."*). Do this once today. The self-monitoring habit is the real prize here.

---

### 4. 📖 Reading for Inference — Stop Reading for Facts
C1 reading tasks, particularly **multiple choice and gapped text**, test whether you understand what a text *implies*, not just what it *states*. Many candidates read too literally.

**Today's micro-task:** Read any two paragraphs of a complex article and write down **two things the author implies but never directly states**. Ask yourself: *What does the writer assume the reader believes? What attitude is revealed by their word choices?* This trains the inferential reading muscle that C1 exams specifically target.

---

### 5. 🧩 Error Pattern Journaling — Make Your Mistakes Work for You
Generic practice without reflection produces slow progress. You need to know *your* specific error patterns.

**Today's micro-task:** Create a simple three-column log: **Error | Why It Happened | Correct Version**. After any practice task today — writing, grammar exercise, speaking — add at least **two entries**. By Week 8, this journal will be your single most valuable revision resource, because it's built entirely from *your* real mistakes, not hypothetical ones.

## ⚠️ One Critical Mistake to Avoid This Week

### ❌ Mistake: Confusing *Complexity* with *Clarity*

At C1, many students start overcomplicating their language — cramming in advanced vocabulary and long sentences — and **lose coherence in the process**. Examiners notice when sophistication is performed rather than natural. A sentence that is long but muddled scores *lower* than a sentence that is precise and well-constructed.

**How to correct it:** Apply the **"So What?" test to every complex sentence you write or speak. After producing an advanced structure, ask: *Is this actually clearer and more precise than a simpler version would be?* If the answer is no, simplify it. C1 is about **controlled sophistication** — complexity in service of meaning, never complexity for its own sake. Aim to be impressive *and* understood, not impressive *instead of* understood.

## ⏱️ Your Daily Habit — Under 10 Minutes, Every Day

### 🌟 The "Three Sentences" Habit

Every evening, before you finish studying, write **exactly three sentences** about any topic — but each sentence must use a **different grammatical structure** you've been practicing that week (e.g., a cleft sentence, a participle clause, a concessive structure).

Example:
- *"It is the consistency of effort, rather than its intensity, that ultimately determines success."* (cleft sentence)
- *"Having underestimated the complexity of the task, many candidates struggle in the final weeks."* (perfect participle clause)
- *"Although technology has transformed communication, the fundamentals of clear expression remain unchanged."* (concessive clause)

This takes **5–8 minutes**. Over the remaining six weeks, you'll have produced **126 sentences** using advanced grammar in context — that's not practice, that's **internalization**. By exam day, these structures will feel natural because they *are* natural to you by then.
