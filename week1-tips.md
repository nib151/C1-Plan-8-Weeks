## 🛠️ Five Specific Improvement Strategies for Week 1

---

### Strategy 1: Build a "Precision Vocabulary" Notebook — Starting Today

At C1, vague language costs marks. Words like *good*, *bad*, *important*, and *said* signal B2 thinking.

**Why it matters:** Examiners reward lexical range and precision. A candidate who writes *"the policy had significant repercussions"* outscores one who writes *"the policy had big effects"* — even if both sentences are grammatically perfect.

**Your micro-task for today:**
Take any 10 words you use frequently in writing or speaking. For each one, find **two C1-level synonyms with different connotations** using a collocation dictionary (Macmillan or Oxford Collocations Dictionary online — both free). Write them in a dedicated notebook with an example sentence you composed yourself.

> *Example: "important" → "pivotal" (turning point context) / "indispensable" (necessity context)*

Do this for 10 words today. Add 5 new words every day this week.

---

### Strategy 2: Read One Quality Article Daily — With Active Annotation

Passive reading builds familiarity. **Active reading builds C1 competence.**

**Why it matters:** C1 reading tasks test your ability to identify implied meaning, authorial attitude, and text organisation — not just surface comprehension. You need to train your brain to read *between* the lines, not just along them.

**Your micro-task for today:**
Go to **The Guardian Long Reads, The Economist, or BBC Future** and choose one article (minimum 600 words). As you read, do the following:

- Underline **one sentence per paragraph** that carries the paragraph's main argument.
- Circle **three words or phrases** you wouldn't have chosen yourself.
- Write a **two-sentence summary** of the author's overall stance — not just the topic.

This takes 15–20 minutes and directly mirrors what C1 reading tasks demand of you.

---

### Strategy 3: Shadow a Native Speaker for 5 Minutes — Every Single Day

Pronunciation and fluency at C1 aren't about accent — they're about **natural rhythm, connected speech, and confident delivery.**

**Why it matters:** In speaking exams, hesitation, unnatural stress patterns, and choppy delivery reduce your score even when your vocabulary is strong. Fluency is a physical skill — it must be practised physically.

**Your micro-task for today:**
Find a **TED Talk or BBC documentary clip** (5 minutes maximum). Listen once normally. Then play it again and **speak along simultaneously**, matching the speaker's pace, pausing, and intonation as closely as possible. Don't worry about perfection — focus on *keeping up*.

Do this once today. Notice where you fall behind — those are your fluency pressure points.

---

### Strategy 4: Write One Paragraph Using a Formal Structure Template

C1 writing isn't just about grammar — it's about **demonstrating control of discourse**. Examiners want to see that you can build an argument, not just express an opinion.

**Why it matters:** Many candidates at this level write fluent but structurally flat paragraphs — a point is made, maybe supported, then the paragraph ends. C1 writing requires **point → evidence/example → analysis → link forward.**

**Your micro-task for today:**
Choose any opinion you hold (on technology, education, environment — anything). Write **one paragraph of 80–100 words** using this exact structure:

1. **Topic sentence** — your clear point
2. **Evidence or example** — specific, not vague
3. **Analysis** — *why* this matters or what it reveals
4. **Linking phrase** — connecting to a broader idea or the next point

Read it back. Ask yourself: *"Does every sentence earn its place?"* Cut anything that doesn't.

---

### Strategy 5: Do a Weekly "Exam Landscape" Audit

You cannot prepare effectively for an exam you don't fully understand.

**Why it matters:** Many C1 students study hard but study *broadly* — they improve their English without specifically improving their exam performance. These are related but not identical goals.

**Your micro-task for today:**
Spend **20 minutes today only** doing the following:

- Look up the exact format of your target C1 exam (number of papers, task types, timing, marking criteria).
- Write down **the three task types you feel least confident about** right now.
- For each one, write one sentence describing *specifically* what makes it feel difficult.

This audit becomes your roadmap. You're not guessing what to study — you're engineering your preparation.

## ⚠️ One Common C1 Mistake to Avoid This Week

### ❌ Mistake: Studying vocabulary in isolated lists

This is the single most common and most damaging habit at C1 level. Students write down words, memorise definitions, and then find they cannot use those words naturally in writing or speaking under pressure.

**Why it happens:** Memorising a word gives you *recognition* — you can identify it when you see it. But C1 requires *production* — you need to retrieve and deploy it accurately in real time.

**How to correct it:**
Never learn a new word without learning it in **at least two collocations and one full sentence you wrote yourself.** When you encounter *"mitigate"*, don't just write *"mitigate = reduce."* Write:

- *"mitigate the risks"* / *"mitigate the impact"*
- *"The government introduced new policies to mitigate the long-term economic impact of the crisis."*

This takes 90 extra seconds per word. Over 8 weeks, it will make your vocabulary dramatically more usable — and examiners will notice.

## ⏱️ Your Daily Habit — Under 10 Minutes, Every Day

### 🔁 The "Three Sentences Before Bed" Habit

Every evening, before you sleep, write **exactly three sentences** in English. That's it.

But here's the rule: **each sentence must use at least one word or structure you encountered that day** — from your reading, your vocabulary notebook, or your shadowing practice.

**Why this works:**
- It forces daily *active retrieval* of new language — the most powerful memory consolidation technique known to learning science.
- It creates a written record of your progress that you can review at Week 4 and Week 8.
- It takes under 5 minutes but signals to your brain that English is a *daily priority*, not a scheduled event.

Over 8 weeks, that's a minimum of **168 sentences** — a personal corpus of C1-level language that you built yourself, one day at a time.
