## 🛠️ Five Specific Improvement Strategies for Week 6

### 1. 🔍 Sharpen Inference Reading — The "Why Behind the Answer" Drill
At C1, reading questions rarely test what the text *says* — they test what it *implies*. The most common error is choosing answers that are factually present in the text but don't answer the actual question asked.

**Do this today:** Take any C1 reading passage (Cambridge C1 Advanced practice papers are ideal). For every answer you select, write one sentence explaining *why the other three options are wrong* — not just why yours is right. This forces you to engage with the distractor logic examiners use and trains you to slow down at the decision point.

---

### 2. ✍️ Upgrade Your Writing Vocabulary — The "Precision Swap" Exercise
C1 writing isn't about using *more* complex words — it's about using *exactly the right* word. Examiners penalise vague high-frequency vocabulary even when the grammar is perfect.

**Do this today:** Take a paragraph you've written recently and highlight every instance of these words: *good, bad, big, important, show, get, make, thing*. Now replace each one with a word that carries a more precise meaning in context. For example:
- *"This shows that..."* → *"This underscores / illustrates / corroborates the notion that..."*
- *"A big problem"* → *"A pervasive / acute / systemic problem"*

Aim to eliminate all eight target words from your active writing vocabulary by exam day.

---

### 3. 🗣️ Build Spoken Lexical Range — The "Three-Register Rule"
Speaking examiners at C1 are explicitly assessing whether you can express the *same idea* in multiple ways without repeating yourself. Most candidates have one register and stay in it.

**Do this today:** Choose one abstract topic (e.g., *the impact of social media on relationships*). Set a 3-minute timer and speak aloud, challenging yourself to express every key idea **three different ways** before moving on. Record yourself. On playback, count how many times you repeated a word unnecessarily. Your target: zero repeated content words within any 60-second window.

---

### 4. 📝 Tackle Use of English — Targeted Collocation Mining
The C1 Use of English section (particularly open cloze and word formation) punishes candidates who know grammar rules but haven't internalised how words *behave together* in natural English.

**Do this today:** Go to a quality English corpus tool (such as *Skell* or the *British National Corpus online*). Search for one word you use frequently — for example, *"concern"* — and study the 10 most common collocations it appears in. Write three original sentences using collocations you didn't previously know. Do this with one new word every day for the remaining weeks.

---

### 5. ⏱️ Simulate Exam Timing — The "Hard Stop" Practice Method
Week 6 is the right moment to stop doing practice tasks with unlimited time. Timing anxiety in the exam room causes more avoidable errors than lack of knowledge.

**Do this today:** Select one full Use of English or Reading section from a past paper. Set a timer for **80% of the official time allowance** — this deliberate pressure trains your brain to make faster, more confident decisions. After the timer stops, review what you completed and what you left. The goal isn't perfection; it's building the mental habit of *committing to answers* rather than hovering.

## ⚠️ One Critical Mistake to Avoid This Week

### ❌ Mistake: Writing Long Sentences to Sound Sophisticated

Many C1 candidates believe that longer, more complex sentences automatically signal higher-level English. Examiners see through this immediately — and worse, long sentences dramatically increase the risk of grammatical errors, unclear referencing, and loss of coherence.

**How to correct it:** Adopt the **"Long-Short-Long" rhythm** in your writing. Follow a complex sentence with a short, punchy one. This demonstrates *control* — which is exactly what C1 assesses — rather than just ambition. For example:

> *"The proliferation of remote working arrangements has fundamentally altered the dynamics of urban planning, prompting city councils to reconsider long-established zoning policies. The implications are profound. Communities that once organised themselves around commuter infrastructure must now reimagine their entire spatial logic."*

Notice how the short middle sentence creates emphasis and shows the writer is in command of their prose — not just filling space.

## 🌅 Your Daily Habit — The 7-Minute Vocabulary Consolidation Ritual

Every evening before you stop studying, spend **exactly 7 minutes** doing this:

1. **Write down 3 new words or phrases** you encountered that day (2 minutes)
2. **Write one original sentence** for each that reflects a C1-level context — not a simple sentence, but one that shows the word in a realistic, nuanced use (3 minutes)
3. **Say each sentence aloud once** with natural stress and intonation (2 minutes)

This habit works because it closes the loop between *passive recognition* and *active production* — the exact gap the C1 exam is designed to expose. Done consistently over the next two weeks, you will add approximately **40–50 high-quality items** to your active vocabulary. That is a measurable, meaningful difference on exam day.
